# Storm Surge and Storm Tide

Storm surge and large waves produced by hurricanes pose the greatest threat
to life and property along the coast. Abnormal water rises can exceed twenty
feet and span hundreds of miles of coastline, causing erosion, structural
destruction and saltwater intrusion well inland. Evacuation planning must
treat storm surge, not wind, as the leading coastal hazard.

# Heavy Rainfall and Inland Flooding

Flooding is the major threat from tropical cyclones for people living
inland, independent of storm surge. Rainfall intensity depends more on a
storm's forward speed and size than on its peak wind category. Anyone
encountering a flooded roadway should turn around rather than attempt to
cross it.

# High Winds

Hurricane-force winds of 74 mph or greater can destroy buildings and mobile
homes, and wind-borne debris becomes a hazard in its own right. Emergency
managers plan evacuations to be complete before the onset of tropical
storm-force winds, not hurricane-force winds, since conditions become unsafe
for evacuation travel well before landfall.

# Rip Currents

Hurricanes generate channeled currents of water flowing away from shore that
can pull even strong swimmers out to sea. These currents, and the associated
drownings and rescues, can occur along a coastline even while a storm remains
over a thousand miles offshore.

# Tornadoes

Tropical cyclones can produce tornadoes embedded in outer rain bands or near
the eyewall. Individual tornadoes are generally weak and short-lived compared
to Great Plains tornadoes, but they remain a significant threat requiring
separate warning response.
